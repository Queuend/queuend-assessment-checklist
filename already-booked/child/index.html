ALREADY BOOKED · CHILDREN & YOUNG PEOPLE

My child’s ADHD assessment is already booked.

If your child or teenager already has an ADHD assessment arranged, this page will help you understand what may happen before, during and after the appointment — including the part played by parents or carers, school or college and the young person themselves.

Every service works a little differently. Treat this as general guidance rather than a description of your child’s particular appointment, and follow any specific instructions from their provider.

01

What happens while we wait?

Check the appointment confirmation carefully and make a note of anything the service has asked you to complete or return beforehand. This might include questionnaires, information from school or college, previous reports, or information from a parent or carer.

If something they have requested is going to be difficult to provide, tell the service rather than trying to fill the gap yourself. It is also worth checking what happens if school information or other forms arrive late, and who to contact if the appointment needs to be changed.

WORTH REMEMBERING

You do not need to spend the waiting period trying to build a case for ADHD. The purpose of the assessment is to understand the whole picture.

02

What should we have ready?

Have whatever the provider has specifically asked for available. Depending on the service, useful information may include:

  • Appointment details: date, time, location or video link
  • School or college reports
  • Information from a SENCo or learning-support team, if available
  • Any questionnaires the service has sent
  • Previous assessments or relevant professional reports
  • A list of current medication and doses
  • Relevant health information
  • Short real-life examples of things you have noticed
  • Your child’s own thoughts about what they find easy and what they find difficult
IF SOMETHING IS MISSING

It can help to think about what happens in more than one setting — for example at home, at school or college, with friends, or during activities outside school.

You do not need to manufacture examples or search for evidence that is no longer available.

If you cannot remember something or do not have an old document, say so. If the service has specifically requested something you cannot provide, let them know and ask whether they need anything else instead.

03

What will happen at the assessment?

There is no single identical process used by every service.

The clinician will usually want to understand your child’s development, current difficulties, how things are going in different parts of life and whether anything else could be contributing.

Information may come from the young person, parents or carers, school or college, previous reports, questionnaires and clinical interviews. Some services may also use additional tests or structured activities, but these do not replace the clinician’s overall assessment.

Depending on the young person’s age and the service, the clinician may speak to the young person themselves as well as to a parent or carer. You can ask beforehand whether any part of the appointment is likely to take place separately.

01

A diagnosis is made

The clinician explains what they have concluded and what happens next.

02

ADHD is not diagnosed

Ask what the clinician thinks may explain the difficulties and what support or further assessment may still be useful.

03

More information is needed

The service may ask for further information or arrange another appointment before reaching a conclusion.

04

How should I prepare my child?

Explain the appointment in straightforward language and make clear that it is not an exam and there are no answers they are supposed to give.

They do not need to “show” the clinician that they have ADHD. Encourage them to describe things as they actually experience them, including anything they find easy, difficult or hard to explain.

It is fine for a young person to say that they do not know, cannot remember, disagree with an adult’s description or experience something differently at school and at home. Those differences can themselves be useful information.

The young person’s own views matter.

Try not to promise that the appointment will result in a particular diagnosis. A useful explanation might simply be that the clinician is trying to understand what is making some things difficult and what support might help.

PRACTICAL NOTE

If your child has particular communication, sensory or accessibility needs, ask the service in advance what adjustments may be possible.

05

What happens afterwards?

Ask when you should expect to hear the outcome and when the written report will be available.

If ADHD is diagnosed

Find out what happens next and whether there will be another appointment to discuss treatment, support or medication if relevant. It is also worth asking who will be responsible for follow-up, reviews and prescribing if medication becomes part of the plan.

If ADHD is not diagnosed

That does not mean the difficulties your child is experiencing are unimportant. Ask what the clinician thinks may explain them, what support may still be useful and whether any further assessment or referral should be considered.

If the outcome is not yet clear

Ask exactly what information is still needed, who is responsible for obtaining it and when you should expect to hear again.

SUPPORT DOES NOT BEGIN WITH A LABEL

A diagnosis is not the only route to support. Some help at school or college can be based on the young person’s needs rather than waiting for a diagnostic label.

06

Useful questions to ask

You will probably not need all of these. Pick the questions that matter to you and keep them somewhere easy to find.

BEFORE

Before the appointment

  • What information do you still need before the assessment?
  • Do you need anything from school or college, and what happens if it is unavailable or arrives late?
  • Who will take part in the assessment?
  • Who makes the final diagnostic decision?
  • Will my child or teenager be spoken to separately for any part of the appointment?
DURING

During the assessment

  • How will my child’s own views be included?
  • What happens if home and school describe things differently?
  • If questionnaires or computer-based tests are used, how do they contribute to the overall assessment?
  • How will you consider other possible explanations or co-occurring conditions?
AFTER

Afterwards

  • When should we expect an outcome?
  • When should we expect the written report?
  • What happens if ADHD is not diagnosed?
  • What happens if the outcome remains unclear?
  • What support might still be useful regardless of the diagnostic outcome?
  • Who should we contact if we have questions afterwards?
