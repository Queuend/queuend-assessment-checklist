ADHD ASSESSMENT ROUTES

NHS, Right to Choose or private?

How to choose an ADHD assessment route — and understand the practical consequences before you commit.

Written by Peter Corkhill, Content Editor. Last reviewed July 2026.

So, you have looked at the patterns and decided they are worth taking seriously. What do you do next?

Unfortunately, the answer is not as straightforward as it ought to be.

The challenge is not simply finding somebody who offers an ADHD assessment. It is working out which route through an unusually complicated system best fits your circumstances — and what happens after the assessment itself.

This guide looks at the main options in England: the local NHS pathway, Right to Choose and private assessment. It also explains some of the questions worth asking before you commit to a provider.

Why the system is difficult

Once someone decides to explore ADHD assessment, they can quickly discover that the system is difficult to navigate.

Awareness of ADHD has risen sharply and demand for assessment has increased with it. NHS services in many areas have struggled to keep pace.

The result is that people may wait a long time for help, often while continuing to experience difficulties at work, school or university, at home or in relationships.

But waiting lists are only part of the problem.

In August 2026, the BBC reported warnings from the NHS Alliance, which represents NHS managers, that ADHD and autism services were at risk of descending into “chaos” as spending rose sharply and the number of providers expanded.

Among the examples gathered from integrated care boards were one area working with 48 providers — four times as many as two years earlier — and another where spending had risen tenfold in a year.

The concern was not simply cost. NHS managers also reported difficulty keeping track of the quality and safety of care across a rapidly growing number of organisations.

That matters for patients too.

More choice does not necessarily mean clearer choices.

NHS England has separately acknowledged significant variation in the prices being paid, the services being specified and the quality being delivered. For 2026/27 it introduced national guide prices for ADHD assessment and treatment pathways in an attempt to bring greater consistency and transparency.

For adult ADHD assessment, those NHS commissioning benchmarks are currently £850 for a face-to-face assessment and £700 for a virtual assessment. The guide price for a face-to-face assessment for a child or young person is £950.

These are not fixed prices and they are not private-patient price lists
. NHS England describes them as non-mandatory benchmarks intended to help commissioners and providers agree appropriate local prices. Individual cases and service models may legitimately cost more or less.

The broader system is also under review. An independent government review is examining rising ADHD, autism and mental-health demand, diagnostic trends and how support and clinical pathways might work differently in future. Its interim findings describe the picture around ADHD diagnosis as more complicated than either simple “overdiagnosis” or “underdiagnosis”.

For someone who needs help now, however, the immediate question remains the same:

Which route should I take, and how do I know what I am actually getting?

The main routes to consider

There is no single pathway that works for everyone.

The most suitable route may depend on:

  • where you live;
  • whether the assessment is for a child or an adult;
  • your local NHS pathway and waiting time;
  • whether Right to Choose is available for the referral you need;
  • whether you can or wish to pay privately;
  • how quickly you need help;
  • and whether medication may later be something you want to explore.

The aim is not to identify a route that guarantees a particular outcome.

No route can do that.

It is to understand the practical differences well enough to make an informed choice.

Local NHS referral through your GP

For many people, the first step is their GP.

You explain your concerns and ask whether referral for an ADHD assessment is appropriate. If the referral is accepted into the local NHS pathway, the assessment is NHS-funded.

The main difficulty can be waiting time.

Availability and pathways differ between adults and children and from one NHS area to another. A quoted waiting time can also mean different things.

It is worth asking:

  • Which service would my referral go to?
  • Is that the local NHS ADHD pathway?
  • What information does the service require?
  • What is its current estimated wait?
  • Is that the wait to first contact, assessment, diagnostic decision or report?
  • What happens after diagnosis if medication is something I want to consider?

A quoted wait to first contact is not necessarily the same thing as the time until a completed assessment.

Right to Choose in England

Right to Choose can be confusing because it is sometimes described as if it sits outside the NHS.

It does not.

Right to Choose is a way in which some patients in England can choose where certain NHS-funded referrals go.

NHS England's current patient-choice guidance confirms that some remotely delivered ADHD assessments may fall within the legal right to provider and team choice. The referring clinician remains responsible for deciding whether a referral is clinically appropriate, but commissioner prior approval is not required where the patient is properly exercising that legal right.

In practice, this can sometimes allow a patient to use a qualifying NHS-contracted provider instead of waiting solely for the usual local service.

This expansion of patient choice has helped create many more ADHD assessment options.

It has also contributed to the complexity now worrying NHS managers.

NHS England says patient choice has resulted in a significant expansion of ADHD and autism assessment providers, alongside wide variation in prices, service specifications and quality.

That does not mean Right to Choose is a poor option.

For many people it can be a very useful one.

It does mean that “NHS-funded” should not be the end of your questions.

Before choosing a Right to Choose provider, find out:

  • whether it is currently accepting the type of referral you need;
  • what the assessment itself includes;
  • who will conduct the assessment;
  • who will make the diagnostic decision;
  • how long you are likely to wait for assessment and report;
  • whether the pathway covers assessment only or also treatment;
  • what happens if medication is recommended;
  • who provides titration;
  • who prescribes during and after titration;
  • what continuing follow-up is provided;
  • and what happens if your GP does not agree to Shared Care.

Right to Choose applies in England
, not throughout the whole UK.

Private assessment

Private assessment can offer a faster route, but it introduces a different set of questions.

Who is actually carrying out the assessment?

Who makes the diagnostic decision?

What does the quoted price include?

How much clinical information is gathered?

Will you receive a detailed written report?

What happens if the conclusion is uncertain?

If medication is recommended, who provides it?

Will there be additional titration, prescription and review charges?

And what happens if your GP later declines Shared Care?

A private diagnosis can be entirely clinically legitimate and extremely useful.

But a quick appointment is not the same thing as a complete and sustainable pathway.

The provider landscape has changed

The growth in demand for ADHD assessment has been accompanied by rapid growth in the organisations offering it.

That has brought benefits. More providers can mean greater capacity, shorter waits and more patient choice.

But it has also made the market harder to understand.

The NHS Alliance findings reported by the BBC suggest that even NHS commissioners are struggling with the pace of expansion, variation in pricing and the task of assuring service quality across increasing numbers of providers.

That makes it even more important for patients to distinguish between:

the organisation selling the assessment;

the clinician conducting it;

the professional responsible for making the diagnostic decision;

and, where medication is involved,

the service responsible for treatment afterwards.

They are not necessarily the same person or organisation.

What does CQC registration tell me?

This is an area where apparently simple advice can be misleading.

It might seem logical to assume that every private ADHD assessment provider in England should appear on the Care Quality Commission register.

That is no longer necessarily the case.

CQC clarified its guidance in March 2026. If an organisation only provides ADHD diagnosis and no treatment
, the diagnostic activity itself does not require CQC registration.

That means absence from the CQC register is not, by itself, evidence that a diagnosis-only ADHD provider is operating improperly.

If the organisation also provides treatment or other regulated activities, the position may be different.

This makes another question particularly important:

Who is the clinician responsible for the diagnostic decision, and what professional registration and specialist ADHD experience do they have?

Regulation of an organisation and professional regulation of an individual clinician are different things.

Do not treat one as a substitute for checking the other.

The private-assessment problem

Private ADHD assessment exists partly because many people feel unable to wait years for answers.

When done well, it can provide timely, careful and sometimes life-changing clarity.

It can also involve a substantial financial commitment.

The private landscape is uneven.

Prices vary, and so can the way assessments are organised.

For ADHD, the pathway after diagnosis can matter every bit as much as the diagnostic appointment.

If medication is recommended, the pathway may involve:

  • a separate medication consultation;
  • titration;
  • follow-up reviews;
  • blood-pressure and pulse monitoring;
  • private prescriptions;
  • medication costs;
  • annual specialist reviews;
  • and a later request for Shared Care with an NHS GP.

This is why comparing only the headline assessment price can be misleading.

A provider that appears inexpensive at the beginning may prove more expensive over the first year if substantial additional charges apply afterwards.

Shared Care

Shared Care is one of the most misunderstood parts of the ADHD pathway.

Broadly, a Shared Care arrangement may allow a specialist to retain responsibility for specialist oversight while a GP takes on agreed prescribing and monitoring once treatment has been stabilised.

But Shared Care is not automatic
.

A GP is not required to accept every Shared Care request.

If Shared Care is declined, a patient who began treatment privately may need to continue paying privately for prescribing, medication reviews and specialist follow-up.

That can turn what appeared to be a one-off assessment cost into a significant continuing expense.

Before committing to a provider, therefore, it is worth asking not simply:

How much is the assessment?

but:

What would this pathway cost me during the first year if Shared Care never happens?

That is a much more useful figure.

What to check before choosing a provider

Whether you are considering Right to Choose or private assessment, find out as much as you reasonably can before committing.

Useful questions include:

  • Who will conduct the assessment?
  • Who will actually make or sign off the diagnostic decision?
  • What professional registration does that clinician hold?
  • What specialist ADHD training and experience do they have?
  • What does the assessment include?
  • What information will I be expected to provide beforehand?
  • What happens if I do not have childhood records or a childhood informant?
  • How are alternative explanations and co-occurring conditions considered?
  • Is the written report included?
  • What will the report contain?
  • What happens if the outcome is unclear?
  • How long should I expect to wait for the assessment, decision and report?
  • What is included in the quoted price?
  • Is medication assessment separate?
  • What does titration cost?
  • Who provides prescriptions?
  • What are the ongoing review costs?
  • What happens if Shared Care is declined?
  • What is the cancellation or refund policy?
  • What regulatory oversight applies to the service?
  • How will my personal information, recordings or AI transcription be used?

You do not need to become an expert in ADHD services before asking for help.

But these questions can reveal important differences between providers that otherwise look very similar.

Getting support without waiting for diagnosis

Assessment is not the only route to help.

Some people can access support through work, university, college or school before a diagnostic assessment has been completed.

Depending on circumstances, that might include:

  • occupational-health support;
  • disability or learning support;
  • reasonable adjustments;
  • classroom changes;
  • exam access arrangements;
  • or Access to Work.

These options are not necessarily substitutes for diagnostic assessment, but they can sometimes address practical difficulties while someone is waiting.

The useful question is:

What support would help me now, regardless of what an eventual assessment concludes?

Making the decision

Deciding whether and where to pursue an ADHD assessment can involve relief, uncertainty, hope and anxiety at the same time.

Good information cannot choose a provider for you.

What it can do is make the decision less opaque.

The questions that matter most are often:

  • What do I hope assessment will change?
  • Which routes are actually available to me?
  • How long am I likely to wait?
  • Who will make the diagnostic decision?
  • What exactly am I paying for?
  • What will the whole first year cost?
  • What happens after diagnosis?
  • What happens if medication is recommended?
  • What happens if Shared Care is refused?
  • What support is available if ADHD is not diagnosed or the outcome is unclear?
  • Am I choosing a complete pathway — or simply the quickest first appointment?

More choice is useful only if people have enough information to make a meaningful choice.

That is particularly important in ADHD care now.
